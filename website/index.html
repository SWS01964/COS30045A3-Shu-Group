Beyond the road toll

Fewer Australians are dying on the roads. The number being seriously hurt hasn’t fallen. We went through a decade of hospital records to find out who is being hurt, and how badly.

By Shu · Published 23 November 2026

Five findings

  1. The road toll is falling. The injury toll isn’t.
  2. Cycling injuries have more than doubled among over-65s.
  3. Pedestrians are 1 in 14 of the injured, but 1 in 4 of those who die in hospital
  4. In the month Australia locked down, crash admissions fell by a third. Cyclist admissions rose.
  5. In 2011 First Nations Australians were hospitalised after road crashes at about the same rate as everyone else. By 2021 the rate was half as high again.
1

Sam asks: Is it getting safer out there?

The road toll is falling. The injury toll isn’t.

Road deaths fell 11.5% between 2011 and 2021, but hospital admissions after road crashes rose 16%, to 39,505. Per 100,000 people the admission rate barely moved (152.3 to 153.8), so there are now 35 admissions for every road death, up from 27.

Hospitalisations are people discharged alive (BITRE Table 1). Road deaths are from the Australian Road Deaths Database. Victoria (2012) and NSW (2017) stopped counting emergency-only cases, which lowers later counts. Per-person rates use ABS population at 30 June. Source: BITRE hospitalised injury data and ARDD; ABS.

2

Sam asks: Are people like me more at risk than I think?

Cycling injuries have more than doubled among over-65s.

Between 2011–13 and 2019–21, yearly admissions of cyclists aged 65–74 rose 138%, and of those aged 75 and over 142%. Allowing for population growth at those ages, the rises are still 78% and 92%, ahead of motorcyclists the same age (64% and 62%).

3

Sam asks: Are my parents more at risk than I think?

Pedestrians are 1 in 14 of the injured, but 1 in 4 of those who die in hospital

In 2011–19 pedestrians were 7.3% of road-crash admissions but 26.4% of the people who died in hospital (538 of 2,041). Age makes it worse: of every 1,000 pedestrians aged 75 and over who were admitted, 61 died, against 28 for all road users that age.

Explore

Deaths in hospital cover 2011–19 (2020–21 are incomplete); bed days and stays cover 2016–21 (counting changed in July 2015). Rates are per 1,000 people admitted, not per head of population, and are shown only with at least 100 cases and 10 deaths; whiskers are 95% intervals. Most road deaths happen at the scene and never reach this data. There is no exposure data. Source: BITRE hospitalised injury data.

4

Sam asks: I started riding in 2020. What happened to people like me?

In the month Australia locked down, crash admissions fell by a third. Cyclist admissions rose.

April 2020 had 2,357 road-crash admissions, 32% fewer than April 2019 and the lowest month in 11 years of data. Every main road user fell except cyclists, up 16%, and cyclist admissions stayed above their 2015–19 range in every month from April to December.

Explore

The grey band is the range of 2015–19. Lockdown dates differed by state. Cyclist admissions were already rising about 3.9% a year: 2020 had 8,332 against a trend of 7,584. Victoria’s cyclist admissions rose 40.5% in July–December 2020, when its second lockdown ran; six-monthly state data can’t show cause. Source: BITRE hospitalised injury data.

5

Sam asks: Are the roads equally safe for everyone?

In 2011 First Nations Australians were hospitalised after road crashes at about the same rate as everyone else. By 2021 the rate was half as high again.

Per 100,000 people, First Nations hospitalisations rose from about 145 in 2011 to 226 in 2021, while the rate for everyone else stayed near 149. The people hurt differ too: pedestrians are 11.0% of First Nations hospitalisations against 6.3% for everyone else, and 24% of First Nations patients live in remote areas, against 1.8%.

Better recording of Indigenous status may explain part of the rise; records with status not stated are left out.

Explore

First Nations population is ABS back-cast estimates (806,090 / 898,515 / 983,709); non-Indigenous is ERP minus First Nations, so it includes people whose status is unknown. Status not stated and n.p. cells are excluded, so shares are of published cells. Remoteness is where the person lives. These are shares, not rates, and there is no exposure data. What to take away

The road toll isn’t the whole story. Road deaths fell 11.5%, but hospital admissions rose 16%, and per person the admission rate barely moved.

Riders are a growing group, especially older ones. Cycling injuries more than doubled among people over 65, and cyclist admissions rose in the month the country locked down while every other road user fell.

For older pedestrians, the danger is what happens after they are admitted. Of every 1,000 pedestrians aged 75 and over who were admitted, 61 died, against 28 for all road users that age.

Per person, the First Nations rate rose, and counts alone hide it. It went from about 145 to 226 per 100,000 while the rate for everyone else stayed near 149. Better recording may explain part of that, and the records can’t say why.

Methods and limitations

Sources

BITRE hospitalised injury data (September 2023 release: national, state and First Nations files, and the data dictionary); ABS estimated resident population (CC BY 4.0); BITRE Australian Road Deaths Database (ARDD fatalities, October 2023); ABS Estimates of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians, June 2021, and the ABS estimates and projections back-cast for 2011 and 2016 (CC BY 4.0): June 2021 estimates and back-cast estimates and projections. The BITRE and ARDD licences are still to be confirmed.

The First Nations population figures (806,090 in 2011; 898,515 in 2016; 983,709 in 2021) come from the ABS back-cast estimates and are not in our CSV files.

Rules

  • Counts are not rates. Per-person figures use ABS population.
  • Headline counts are hospitalisations (people discharged alive), as in BITRE Table 1. Death rates use everyone admitted.
  • Deaths in hospital: 2011–2019 only (2020–21 records are incomplete). Bed days: 2016–2021 only (counting changed in July 2015). Average stay is days per person who left hospital alive.
  • Rates are shown only where there are at least 100 cases and 10 deaths. Suppressed cells (n.p.) are treated as missing, never as zero.
  • Victorian (2012) and NSW (2017) counting changes lower later counts.
  • First Nations figures leave out records with Indigenous status not stated, and recording may have improved over the decade.
  • Remoteness and state are where the patient lives, not where the crash happened.
  • No exposure data (trips, distance): we cannot say which way of travelling is riskier per trip.
