Beyond the road toll

Fewer Australians are dying on the roads. The number being seriously hurt hasn’t fallen. We went through a decade of hospital records to find out who is being hurt, and how badly.

By ShuPublished 23 November 2026

Chapter 1 · The hidden road toll

The road toll is falling. The injury toll isn’t.

The question: Is it getting safer out there?

What to know

  • 39,505 people were admitted to hospital after road crashes in 2021, about 108 a day.
  • Since 2011, road deaths are down 11.5% and hospital admissions are up 16%.
  • Per person, the hospitalisation rate barely moved: about 152 to 154 per 100,000.

Each year Australia publishes its road toll. In 2021 it was 1,130 deaths, down from 1,277 in 2011. But deaths are only the visible part. In 2021, 39,505 people were admitted to hospital with injuries from a road crash. That is about 108 people every day.

Deaths and hospitalisations are very different sizes, so they cannot share one axis. The chart below sets both to 100 in 2011, so the gap between the lines is the story.

Counts alone are not a fair test: Australia has 15% more people than in 2011, so more of everything is expected. Switch to Per person and the picture is clear. The death rate fell from 5.72 to 4.40 per 100,000 people, a fall of almost a quarter (−23%). The hospitalisation rate did not move: 152 per 100,000 in 2011 and 154 in 2021.

108

people a day were admitted to hospital after a road crash in 2021, for about three road deaths a day.

Fewer people are dying on our roads. The same number of people, per head, are still being badly hurt. The true rise may be larger than shown: New South Wales and Victoria changed how they count cases (see Methods and limitations), and both changes lower the counts.

Chapter 2 · Road injury is getting older

Older riders, longer stays: cycling injuries have more than doubled among over-65s.

The question: Are people like me more at risk than I think?

What to know

  • Cyclist admissions rose 138% among 65–74 year-olds and 142% among those aged 75 and over (3-year averages).
  • Allowing for population growth, the rises are still 78% and 92% per person.
  • People aged 75 and over stay 11.1 days in hospital on average, against 4.3 for 26–39 year-olds.

The national total hides who is being hurt. The chart below compares the first three years of data (2011–13) with the last three (2019–21), by road user and age. Using three-year averages keeps one unusual year from skewing the result. Orange cells are rises, blue cells are falls.

Take pedal cyclists aged 65–74. Their hospitalisations rose 138%, and for those aged 75 and over, 142%. Part of this is because there are more older Australians: the 65–74 group grew 33% and the 75+ group 26%. Even per person, the rises are 78% and 92%. Cyclists aged 40–64, a much bigger group, rose 45%.

+142%

rise in cyclists aged 75 and over admitted to hospital, 2011–13 to 2019–21 (3-year averages).

One caution: the data has no measure of how much people ride. More older Australians may be cycling more, which would raise injuries without making cycling riskier per trip.

When older people are hurt, the consequences are heavier. They are admitted less often than younger adults, but they stay in hospital longer and are far more likely to die there.

Chapter 3 · Not every crash is equal

Pedestrians are 1 in 14 of the injured, but 1 in 4 of those who die in hospital

Counting admissions treats a sprained wrist and a fractured skull the same. Look at what happens after admission and a different road user comes to the top of the list.

The question: Are my parents more at risk than I think?

What to know

  • Pedestrians were 7.3% of road-crash admissions, but 26.4% of the people who died in hospital (2011–2019).
  • Of every 1,000 pedestrians admitted, about 22 died in hospital. For car drivers it was about 6, and for cyclists about 2.
  • For pedestrians aged 75 and over it was 61 in 1,000, and survivors stayed in hospital for an average of 19 days.

If you only count admissions, car drivers and motorcyclists dominate the picture, and cyclists are now the third-largest group. Pedestrians barely register: about one admission in 15.

Count the days people spend in hospital and the order shifts. Between 2016 and 2021 pedestrians made up 6.6% of admissions but 13.1% of bed days. Count the people who did not survive their stay and the gap widens again. From 2011 to 2019, pedestrians were 7.3% of admissions and 26.4% of in-hospital deaths: 538 of 2,041.

Our per-person figures for people aged 75 and over drift down, though part of that comes from a change in how New South Wales counts cases in 2017. What changes is the outcome.

A pedestrian aged 75 or over who is admitted after being hit dies in hospital about 61 times in every 1,000. Across all road users of that age it is 28. Those who survive stay an average of 19 days, against 11 for older road users generally. Pedestrians aged 65 and over make up fewer than a quarter of injured pedestrians, but more than half of the pedestrians who die in hospital.

61 in 1,000

pedestrians aged 75 and over admitted after being hit died in hospital, 2011–2019.

For every group of road users we could measure reliably, being hit by a heavy vehicle or bus carried the highest death rate. For pedestrians it was about 53 per 1,000 admitted. Collisions with trains were worse again, but so rare that the figure rests on 17 deaths.

A caution on this chapter. These figures describe what happens to people once they reach hospital. Most road deaths happen at the scene and never appear in this data.

Chapter 4 · The lockdown test

In the month Australia locked down, crash admissions fell by a third. Cyclist admissions rose.

In 2020 Australians travelled less, worked from home and bought bikes. Hospital records show how that played out on the roads.

The question: I started riding in 2020. What happened to people like me?

What to know

  • April 2020 had 2,357 road-crash admissions, 32% fewer than April 2019 and the lowest month of the decade.
  • In that month admissions fell for every main road user except cyclists, which rose 16%.
  • From April to December 2020, cyclist admissions beat every same month of the previous five years.

Australia’s first national restrictions came in late March 2020. In April, 2,357 people were admitted to hospital after road crashes. That’s 1,131 fewer than in April 2019, and the lowest monthly figure in the 11 years of data.

The fall was steepest for people on foot. Pedestrian admissions fell by 60%, car passengers by 53%, drivers by 43% and motorcyclists by 38%. One line went the other way.

Cyclist admissions rose 16% in April and kept climbing. June 2020 had 682, half as many again as June 2019. Over the whole year, cyclist admissions went from 7,302 to 8,332, up 14%, while pedestrian admissions fell 22% and car drivers 9%.

Children were a big part of it. Riders aged 8 to 16 made up one in 7 injured cyclists in 2019 but almost a third of the 2020 increase.

+16%

change in cyclist admissions, April 2020 on April 2019, in a month when every other main road user fell by at least a third.

Cycling injuries had been rising for years, so not all of 2020’s increase is a lockdown effect. Projecting the 2011–2019 trend forward gives about 7,670 admissions for 2020; the real figure was 8,332. That’s 662 more than the trend, within the normal year-to-year swing on its own. Month by month the signal is clearer: from April to December, every month came in above what the trend predicted.

We can’t say how many more people were riding, because there is no national count of trips or kilometres. The likeliest explanation is more riders on the road, not riding becoming more dangerous, but this data cannot prove it.

A caution on this chapter. Restrictions differed by state. Victoria’s second lockdown fits the timing of the biggest state rise in cyclist admissions (July–December 2020, +40.5%), but six-monthly state data cannot show cause and effect.

What to take away

Three things to remember

The road toll isn’t the whole story. Deaths have fallen, but the number of Australians seriously hurt has stayed the same per person: more than 100 admissions a day in 2021.

If you ride, you’re part of a growing group. Cycling admissions rose faster than any other group’s over the decade, more than doubled among people over 65, and rose again when the country locked down. None of this shows that riding got more dangerous per trip. It does show that more riders are ending up in hospital, and that older riders are a growing share of them.

If your parents walk, the danger isn’t how often they’re hit but what happens when they are. Older pedestrians aren’t admitted more often than they were, but when they are, about one in 16 of those aged 75 and over doesn’t come home.

Methods and limitations

This story uses open government data on people admitted to hospital after a road crash in Australia, 2011–2021. Keep these rules in mind when reading it.

Rules we followed

  • Counts are not rates. Counts rise when a population grows. Per-person figures divide by ABS population (per 100,000 residents in Chapter 1; by each age group’s growth in Chapter 2). Chapter 3’s “per 1,000” figures are per 1,000 people admitted, not per head of population.
  • Deaths in hospital: 2011–2019 only. Records for 2020 and 2021 look incomplete. Hospitalisation counts exclude people who died in hospital; road deaths come from a separate database.
  • Bed days: 2016–2021 only. The counting method changed in July 2015. Average stay excludes people who died in hospital.
  • Two counting changes lower the counts. From 2012 Victoria stopped including cases that only visited an emergency department; from June 2017 New South Wales made a similar change. The national rise is probably understated, and the NSW dip after 2017 is a counting effect, not a safety gain.
  • Residence, not crash location. State and remoteness refer to the patient’s usual home.
  • No exposure data. The data does not say how much people drive, ride or walk, so a rise in injuries could reflect more travel, more risk, or both.
  • Small numbers. Changes based on fewer than about 30 cases are greyed out. Death rates are published only where there are at least 100 cases and 10 deaths; average stays only where there are at least 100 cases.

Processing

Raw BITRE tables were cleaned and aggregated with Python scripts and checked against BITRE’s published summary tables; a KNIME workflow reproduces the key figures. Code and validation notes are in the project’s processing folder.

What we left out

A chapter on equity (First Nations people and remoteness) is planned but not published. The First Nations tables don’t add up to the national totals by more than suppressed cells can explain, and we won’t publish findings we can’t reconcile.
